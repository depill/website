Use this as a blank starting point for a process-mapping conversation. It is a template, not a description of a particular company’s workflow.

## Start with the boundaries

- **Purpose:** What outcome should this process produce?
- **Trigger:** What starts it?
- **End:** What tells us it is complete?
- **Owner:** Who is responsible for the outcome?
- **Scope:** What is included, and what belongs to another process?

Keep the first version small enough to explain in one conversation.

## Write down the steps

Capture what happens today, including the work outside the main system.

| Step                     | Person or team   | System | Input           | Output             |
| ------------------------ | ---------------- | ------ | --------------- | ------------------ |
| 1. Describe the action   | Who does it?     | Where? | What is needed? | What changes?      |
| 2. Describe the handover | Who receives it? | Where? | What arrives?   | What happens next? |

## Look at the exceptions

The ordinary route is only part of the picture. For each step, ask:

- What can be missing or incorrect?
- Who notices, and how?
- Who decides what to do next?
- Can someone retry the action safely?
- What evidence is kept?

## Separate observations from ideas

Keep the current process and proposed changes distinct. A useful note can be as simple as:

```text
Observation:
Evidence or example:
Effect on the next step:
Proposed change:
Question still to answer:
```

## Agree on one next step

Choose a specific part of the process to investigate or improve. Record who owns that step and what evidence will show whether the change helped.

A process map can stay useful without becoming complicated. Update it when the work changes.
